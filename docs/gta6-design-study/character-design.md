# Character Design: leads and crowds

## 1. The leads: a relationship, not a roster

Jason Duval (ex-military, drug running) and Lucia Caminos (out of prison, a fighter) are a
**couple**, not strangers assembled for a heist. Players can move between them freely —
running together as a pair, or splitting off to act independently.

That single choice reorganises character design:

- **Silhouettes must work as a pair.** The cover art and key art stage them as a two-shot;
  the design problem is a duo composition, not two separate hero shots.
- **The bond is the stake.** In a heist crew, tension comes from betrayal. In a couple, it
  comes from intimacy, which is a different animation, dialogue and framing budget —
  domestic scenes, an apartment, choosing a dress for cocktails, rom-com beats braided into
  crime.
- **Performance detail is the sell.** The examples that landed publicly are small and
  human: a brow furrowing under a bench press, hair that behaves like hair. Micro-expression
  and secondary motion do more for character than costume complexity.

**Transferable rule:** decide the relationship first. It determines the framing, the
animation set, and the entire tone before a single costume is drawn.

## 2. Illustration language

Key art uses the series' long-running American comic-book / airbrushed-poster idiom:
flat-lit figures with hard outlines, saturated grade, staged against a skyline with palms
and a helicopter. The game renders photoreally; the *marketing* is illustrated. Those two
registers are kept deliberately separate, and the illustration is what people can draw from
memory.

**Transferable rule:** your promotional language does not have to be your rendering
language. A reproducible illustrated identity travels further than screenshots.

## 3. Crowds: appearance as evidence

Rockstar describes GTA VI's population as the most detailed and varied it has built, with
new tools and pipelines developed specifically for it — reportedly with an entire studio
working on NPCs, and a hired stylist dressing the population.

The axes of variation actually named:

- **Body** — age, height, build, weight; tattoos, piercings, visible cosmetic surgery.
- **Clothing** — throwback Hawaiian shirts, sharp suits, luxury labels, muddy combat gear.
- **Behaviour** — animation sets, daily routines, and how crowds are *placed* per location.

The organising idea is that **appearance is contextual evidence**. Residents of Little Cuba
look and dress unlike people out by Mount Kalaga National Park. Clothing encodes taste,
lifestyle, affluence and individuality — and, in Rockstar's framing, "Vice City is built
around vanity", so the fashion ecosystem is thematic argument as much as variety.

**Transferable rule:** tie every appearance parameter to a location or a life circumstance.
Random variation reads as noise; correlated variation reads as a society.

## 4. The player's body as a record of play

The lead characters extend Red Dead Redemption 2's transformation systems into something
closer to a lifestyle simulation:

- Diet changes weight.
- Exercise builds and tones visible muscle.
- Sleep deprivation — after a long police chase or a bender — visibly changes the face.
- Haircuts, outfits, fitness, food and rest all read on the model.

The stated intent: *"We want to enable as much self-expression as possible, without losing
the essence of the characters."* That is the hard constraint in any customisation system —
the player must be able to change the character without being able to destroy them.

**Transferable rule:** make the character's body the game's status display. Visible state
beats a stat screen, and it means the player's history is legible in every cutscene.

## 5. Research as a character pipeline

The population's credibility is sourced, not invented: years of trips to Miami and its
surrounding communities, plus interviews with ex-criminals, law enforcement, club promoters
and gun dealers connected to Florida's culture. The specificity in dress, speech and posture
comes from those rooms.

**Transferable rule:** talk to the people you are depicting. It is the cheapest possible
source of the details that no amount of reference-board scrolling will produce.
