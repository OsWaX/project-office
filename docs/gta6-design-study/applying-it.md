# Applying It: the transferable principles

Condensed checklists. Each item is a design decision you can make on your own project
today, independent of budget or medium.

## World / city

- [ ] **Define regions by subtraction.** For every region, finish the sentence "this is the
      place without ___". If two regions answer the same way, cut one.
- [ ] **Pick a real place and study it properly.** Photographs, maps, street view, and if
      possible a visit. Recognition by a local is the bar.
- [ ] **Transpose, don't invent.** One-to-one on silhouettes people recognise; rename what
      is owned; compress geography that hurts traversal; parody what is already absurd.
- [ ] **Give each district four channels of difference** — architecture, surface writing,
      population, traffic/props. Three minimum, or it's a filter.
- [ ] **Place density deliberately.** Where the crowd *isn't* defines the city as much as
      where it is.
- [ ] **Systems shared, data varied.** No bespoke code per location.

## Light and colour

- [ ] **Author two opposed grades** for the same geometry — and don't reconcile them.
- [ ] **Mix at least three light sources per hero scene**, each with its own colour
      temperature.
- [ ] **Let light colour its surroundings** — bounce, tinted wet surfaces, visible shafts
      through humid air.
- [ ] **Plant specular anchors** — a reflective vest, a chrome bumper, sweat. Cheap, and it
      sells the whole lighting model.
- [ ] **Allow expressionist lighting** where mood beats plausibility.
- [ ] **Identify your flat hour** (usually high noon) and design around showing less of it.
- [ ] **Consider carrying period through optics** — grain, flare, lens character — rather
      than through set dressing.

## People

- [ ] **Establish the central relationship before the character sheets.** It sets tone,
      framing, and the animation budget.
- [ ] **Design silhouettes as a group composition**, not as isolated hero renders.
- [ ] **Spend on micro-performance** — brow, breath, hair, weight shift — before spending on
      costume complexity.
- [ ] **Correlate appearance with place and circumstance.** Neighbourhood, income, trade,
      age, and taste should all be readable off a stranger.
- [ ] **Name your variation axes explicitly** — age, height, build, modification, garment
      tier, routine — and make sure the pipeline can express each one.
- [ ] **Hire or consult a stylist.** Clothing is a specialist discipline and it shows when
      it isn't.
- [ ] **Make the body the status display.** Player behaviour — food, sleep, training,
      violence — should be visible on the model.
- [ ] **Bound customisation by character essence.** Self-expression up to, not through, the
      character's identity.
- [ ] **Interview real people** from the world you are depicting.

## Brand and surface

- [ ] **Treat in-world copy as a discipline** with its own writer: brand names, slogans,
      packaging, billboards, signage.
- [ ] **Use branding as the satire layer** so dialogue doesn't have to carry the commentary.
- [ ] **Make the logo do more than one job** — mark, monogram, and container.
- [ ] **Keep marketing illustration distinct from in-game rendering.** The illustrated
      identity is what people remember and reproduce.

## Anti-patterns visible by contrast

- One global colour grade applied to every hour and every region.
- Districts that differ only in building textures.
- Uniform crowd density everywhere.
- NPC variety generated by random shuffling of parts, uncorrelated with place.
- Signage modelled but not *written*.
- Customisation systems that let the player erase the character.
